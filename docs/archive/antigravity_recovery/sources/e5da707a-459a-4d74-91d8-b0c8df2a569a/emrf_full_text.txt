[014] (Heading 1): 1. Research Context and Origin
[015] (Normal): The project began from a top-down conceptual question: whether matter should be regarded as fundamental or whether matter could emerge from a deeper spacetime description. The working hypothesis treats matter as an emergent state associated with a sufficiently strong or otherwise structured state of spacetime.
[016] (Normal): The term 'compression' is intentionally used as a meta-level visual and conceptual descriptor. The project does not yet equate compression with any single established curvature scalar, energy density, or thermodynamic variable. The computational program is intended to determine whether a mathematically precise compression functional can be constructed from measurable or derivable quantities.
[017] (Normal): Visual analogies—pressure, heat, cellular turnover, biological evolution, and the eventual transformation of organized systems—are useful hypothesis-generation and communication devices. They are not themselves evidence. The research record must separate visualization, interpretation, mathematical derivation, and empirical evidence.

[021] (Heading 1): 3. Mathematical Core — Current Status
[022] (Heading 2): 3.1 Working matter relation
[023] (Normal): M(X,t) = k [ C(X,t) / C0 ]^alpha
[024] (Normal): This is a phenomenological scaling ansatz. The normalization makes the exponent dimensionless if C and C0 share units. The physical units of M and k depend on whether M is defined as mass density, effective energy density, invariant matter content, or another observable.
[025] (Heading 2): 3.2 Generalized coordinate
[026] (Normal): X = {x, y, z, d1, d2, ..., dn}
[027] (Normal): The generalized coordinate preserves ordinary spatial position while allowing additional dimensional/state degrees of freedom. It must eventually be formalized as a point or state on a manifold, fiber bundle, extended configuration space, or another defensible structure.
[028] (Heading 2): 3.3 Compression as a derived functional
[029] (Normal): C(X,t) → F[gμν, R, Rμν, Rμνρσ, Tμν, S, ...]
[030] (Normal): The central mathematical problem is to derive or identify F. Candidate inputs include the metric, curvature invariants, geodesic deviation/convergence, stress-energy, local energy density, horizon properties, and entropy/information measures. Model selection must penalize unnecessary complexity and be tested out of sample.
[031] (Heading 2): 3.4 Space and entropy
[032] (Normal): The latest conceptual statement is: 'space and entropy define compression.' The scientifically useful form is a hypothesis about a functional rather than an asserted algebraic sum:
[033] (Normal): C = F(gμν, S, ∇S, curvature invariants, Tμν, ...)
[034] (Normal): The entropy symbol S cannot remain ambiguous. Thermodynamic entropy, coarse-grained statistical entropy, von Neumann entropy, entanglement entropy, and black-hole entropy are not interchangeable. Each experiment must specify its entropy definition and justification.
[035] (Heading 1): 4. Relationship to Established Physics
[036] (Normal): The framework must reproduce established results in regimes where General Relativity, Newtonian gravity, thermodynamics, and quantum theory have been tested. Conceptual connections to black-hole thermodynamics motivate investigation but do not validate CSTMH.
[037] (Normal): Jacobson's 1995 work is directly relevant because it derived Einstein's equation as an equation of state from horizon entropy proportional to area together with δQ = TδS. This establishes an important precedent for relating geometry and thermodynamics, not evidence that CSTMH is correct.
[038] (Normal): Black-hole entropy and entanglement-entropy research provide additional constraints and motivation. They establish deep relationships among horizons, geometry, information, and thermodynamics, but do not establish the proposed compression framework.
[039] (Heading 1): 5. The Real-Data Stellar Experiment
[040] (Normal): The original empirical intent should be retained: use real stellar observations around a black hole to reconstruct multiple trajectories through a common gravitational field and test whether the proposed compression framework yields measurable predictions beyond or equivalent to established GR.
[041] (Heading 2): 5.1 Primary target: Sagittarius A*
[042] (Normal): Sagittarius A* is the preferred benchmark because decades of infrared observations establish multiple stellar orbits around the Galactic Center's compact central mass. ESO/VLT/VLTI and complementary Keck/Gemini observations provide the strongest relevant data.
[043] (Heading 2): 5.2 Three-star experiment
[044] (Normal): The three-star concept should be preserved, but star selection should be driven by data quality, orbital diversity, and public/reproducible provenance.
[045] (List Bullet): Historical benchmark: S1, S2, and S8. These three were explicitly analyzed together in early Galactic Center orbit work, preserving the historical three-trajectory concept.
[046] (List Bullet): Modern benchmark: S2, S29, and S38. A 2022 GRAVITY analysis provides extensive 1992–2021 coverage for these stars. S55 can be retained as a fourth validation trajectory.
[047] (Normal): The modern benchmark is preferable for the first computational experiment because its published observational coverage is extensive and includes high-precision interferometric data. The historical S1/S2/S8 set remains valuable as an independent methodological cross-check.
[048] (Heading 2): 5.3 What 'triangulation' means scientifically
[049] (Normal): The experiment should not claim that three stars literally triangulate the event horizon. Rather, multiple independent stellar trajectories provide multiple probes of a common spacetime potential. Their positions, accelerations, radial velocities, orbital orientations, pericenters, and relativistic residuals jointly constrain the model. 'Triangulation' is therefore an intuitive description of multi-trajectory constraint.
